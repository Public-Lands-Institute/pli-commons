## Visual Inquiry

The PLI black-and-white photography of Pointe Mouillee records a landscape whose geometry is overtly artificial. Straight dike lines bisect the frame horizontally. Water cells are compartmentalized into units of management rather than ecological flow. The photographs read as infrastructure documentation as much as landscape photography.

The tonal range within individual cells is forensically informative: dark, flat water surfaces indicate shallow impoundments with low biological activity; graduated gray transitions along shoreline margins record the texture of consolidated dredge spoil — a material that does not behave like natural soil under compression or root colonization. Vegetation in the imagery is patterned rather than distributed, reflecting mechanical seeding regimes rather than natural recruitment.

Use the photographic record to:
- Identify the geometric linearity of CDF dike construction versus any residual natural shoreline morphology.
- Compare vegetative texture between active impoundment cells and cells in various stages of consolidation management.
- Read water turbidity variation across cells as an index of sediment consolidation progress.

## Ecological Forensic Report

**Site classification:** Confined Disposal Facility (CDF); managed waterfowl impoundment.

**Land use history:**
Pointe Mouillee exists at the intersection of two regulatory systems: Great Lakes navigation maintenance (which generates dredge spoil) and waterfowl management (which requires shallow-water impoundments). The CDF framework resolves the logistics of both by constructing artificial wetland cells from the same toxic material that navigation dredging produces.

The dredge spoil deposited at Pointe Mouillee is not ecologically neutral fill. Detroit River and western Lake Erie navigation channel sediments accumulated decades of heavy-metal, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH), and polychlorinated biphenyl (PCB) loading from Downriver industrial operations. The CDF dike system contains this material while it consolidates — a process requiring decades — and the surface is then managed as marsh habitat before full consolidation is achieved.

The resulting ecology is built on a contaminated substrate beneath a layer of managed habitat. Wildlife using the impoundments — particularly diving ducks and shorebirds that probe sediment — encounter contamination at depth. The CDF perimeter structures (rock-armor dikes, navigational aids, access roads) define the outer boundary of the site's material composition.

**Key forensic indicators:**
- Geometric cell boundaries: CDF construction artifact, not natural marsh feature.
- Uniform low-relief topography across impoundments: characteristic of consolidated dredge spoil.
- *Phragmites australis* monocultures along dike margins: indicator of disturbed, nutrient-enriched substrate.
- Absence of native sedge meadow or wet prairie transition zones: consistent with artificial substrate origin.

**Active processes:**
Ongoing dredge spoil placement continues at Pointe Mouillee as a component of federal navigation maintenance. The site therefore exists in a permanent state of construction — new cells are periodically added as dredge disposal need outpaces consolidation.

## Shadow History

Pointe Mouillee — "wet point" in French — marks the location where the Huron River historically discharged into western Lake Erie across a broad delta of shoals, emergent marsh, and open water. This delta was a primary fishery for Potawatomi, Wyandot, and Ottawa communities operating along the Erie shoreline, and a significant node in the inter-tribal water transportation network linking the Detroit River, Lake Erie, and inland river systems.

The French toponym preserves a European-era geographic description while erasing the prior Indigenous place-naming system. Wyandot (Wendat) communities maintained villages in the Huron River drainage immediately north of the point. Ottawa and Potawatomi communities used the Erie littoral corridor seasonally.

The 1807 Treaty of Detroit ceded the Erie shoreline, including the Huron River mouth, to the United States. The productive marsh and shoal environment at Pointe Mouillee — which had sustained Indigenous economies for millennia — was subsequently drained, diked, and converted to agricultural use in the nineteenth century, then partially re-inundated in the twentieth as part of waterfowl management programs. The current CDF construction represents the most recent phase of this land-use transformation sequence.

The contaminated dredge spoil that now constitutes the substrate of Pointe Mouillee's managed wetlands is material extracted from the bottom of navigation channels cut through what was formerly intact marsh and shoal habitat — the same habitat type that sustained the pre-dispossession Indigenous economy of the site.

**Treaty reference:** Treaty of Detroit (1807).

**Nations:** Potawatomi (Bodéwadmik), Wyandot (Wendat), Ottawa (Odawa).

## Inquiry Prompts

1. **The CDF as palimpsest:** A Confined Disposal Facility resolves two bureaucratic problems simultaneously — where to deposit dredge spoil and how to create waterfowl habitat — using the same contaminated material. Analyze the CDF as a spatial and regulatory technology: what labor systems does it obscure, what ecological risks does it contain rather than remediate, and how does the photographic documentation of its geometric cell structure differ from the documentation of a natural wetland?

2. **Toponymy and erasure:** The name "Pointe Mouillee" is French and preserves a colonial-era geographic description while effacing the Indigenous place-name for the Huron River delta. Research the naming conventions applied to Great Lakes shoreline features in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. What does the persistence of French rather than Wyandot or Ottawa place-names in this region reveal about whose knowledge systems were legible to the cartographic record, and whose were not?

3. **Navigation infrastructure and ecological debt:** The dredge spoil at Pointe Mouillee originated from navigation channel maintenance — industrial work that deepened shipping lanes by removing sediment from the Detroit River and western Lake Erie. Trace the chain of consequence: what commercial and industrial interests drove navigation dredging; what sediment contaminants accumulated in that process; and how does the containment of that contaminated material at Pointe Mouillee represent a transfer of ecological cost from the navigation economy to the wildlife management system?
