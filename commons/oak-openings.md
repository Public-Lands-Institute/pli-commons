## Visual Inquiry

The PLI black-and-white photography at Oak Openings documents the central visual problem of the site: the tension between the geometric regularity of Civilian Conservation Corps (CCC) Red Pine plantation rows and the irregular, open-canopy structure of native oak savanna. These two landscape types are legible in the photographic record as distinct tonal regimes. Red Pine plantations produce a dense, uniform vertical pattern — parallel dark trunks in regular spacing, canopy closure suppressing understory light penetration. Native oak openings produce an irregular branching pattern, open sky between canopy crowns, and a complex understory texture of bluestem grass and sand-adapted forbs.

The photographs are a record of a landscape in managed retreat from its CCC-era plantation phase. Where pine removal has occurred, the frame opens; where plantations remain, the frame closes into the regular vertical rhythm of an industrial forestry aesthetic.

Use the photographic record to:
- Identify plantation-phase Red Pine stands by canopy regularity and trunk spacing geometry.
- Locate post-removal restoration areas by reading increased light penetration and understory recruitment texture.
- Map sand substrate exposure (the distinguishing ecological feature of Oak Openings) by reading bare soil patches in open-canopy zones.

## Ecological Forensic Report

**Site classification:** Oak savanna / sand barren ecosystem; CCC-era pine plantation overlay; active restoration to pre-plantation condition.

**Land use history — pre-CCC:**
Oak Openings is a relic landscape defined by its substrate: an Ankokan sand ridge deposited by Lake Erie's ancestral beach lines, producing an excessively drained, low-nutrient sand plain that supports plant communities adapted to drought, fire, and low soil fertility. The native ecosystem — globally rare oak savanna with *Quercus velutina* and *Quercus alba* over a matrix of big bluestem (*Andropogon gerardii*), little bluestem (*Schizachyrium scoparium*), and sand-adapted forbs — was maintained by a fire regime.

By the early twentieth century, fire suppression, agricultural conversion of adjacent land, and selective timber harvest had significantly degraded the native oak savanna. The remaining sand plain was perceived by federal land agencies as waste ground: too dry and infertile for sustained agriculture, producing no commercially valuable timber.

**CCC intervention (1930s–1940s):**
The Civilian Conservation Corps addressed the degraded Oak Openings landscape through the standard federal reforestation template of the New Deal era: Red Pine (*Pinus resinosa*) and Scots Pine (*Pinus sylvestris*) plantations installed in geometric grid patterns across the sand plain. This was reforestation as industrial production logic — species selected for growth rate and timber value, not ecological fit; spacing determined by mechanical planting operations; the plantation conceived as a factory floor.

Red Pine is not native to northwestern Ohio. The plantation grid imposed on the Oak Openings sand plain accelerated the loss of native understory diversity by creating dense, continuous canopy that suppressed the light-demanding oak savanna community.

**Current restoration trajectory:**
Metro Parks has undertaken systematic Red Pine removal across Oak Openings since the late 1990s, using mechanical harvest, chainsaw crews, and prescribed fire to reestablish oak savanna structure. The process is slow: pine root mats alter soil structure, and invasive species (*Carduus* spp., *Centaurea* spp.) exploit disturbed substrates ahead of native recruitment.

**Key forensic indicators:**
- Regular trunk spacing in plantation zones: CCC installation artifact.
- Abrupt canopy transition between plantation and native/restored zones.
- Exposed sand substrate in open-canopy areas: the native soil surface, now being recovered.
- Prescribed burn scars on oak bark: evidence of active fire management to maintain savanna structure.

## Shadow History

The Oak Openings sand plain was Council Fire territory — the Ottawa (Odawa) and Potawatomi (Bodéwadmik) nations maintained significant presence across the Maumee River corridor and adjacent uplands, with Council Fire assemblies serving as the diplomatic and political framework for inter-tribal governance in the western Lake Erie region. Wyandot communities were also present in the broader Maumee drainage.

The oak savanna and sand barren ecosystem was not a landscape that existed independently of human management. The fire regime that maintained open canopy and prevented forest succession was sustained in significant part through deliberate burning practices by Indigenous communities — the same management technique used across the eastern deciduous forest margin to maintain hunting grounds, travel corridors, and berry-producing edge habitats. The oak savanna's distinctive ecology is, in part, an artifact of pre-contact Indigenous land management.

The 1817 Treaty of Maumee Rapids and the 1833 Treaty of Chicago ceded the northwestern Ohio and northeastern Indiana territories — including the Maumee River drainage and the Oak Openings sand plain — to the United States. Ottawa, Potawatomi, and associated nations were removed westward in the years following, with most Ohio Ottawa removed to Kansas by the early 1840s.

Fire suppression — which began degrading the oak savanna structure almost immediately after removal — was not merely a management failure. It was the erasure of a specific land-management practice that could no longer be maintained once the people who practiced it had been expelled. The landscape that the CCC inherited as "waste ground" in the 1930s was a system already one century into ecological decline following the removal of its active managers.

The current restoration program — prescribed burning, invasive removal, pine harvest — is reconstructing, with institutional labor and public funding, a management regime that was maintained for centuries by the nations dispossessed from the land.

**Treaty references:** Treaty of Maumee Rapids (1817); Treaty of Chicago (1833).

**Nations:** Ottawa (Odawa), Potawatomi (Bodéwadmik), Wyandot (Wendat).

## Inquiry Prompts

1. **The CCC plantation as colonial technology:** The Civilian Conservation Corps installed Red Pine grids at Oak Openings using a reforestation template derived from European industrial forestry — species selection by growth rate and timber value, geometric spacing for mechanical operations, monoculture canopy as the production goal. Analyze how this model of "improvement" applied to an Indigenous-managed landscape reproduces colonial assumptions about which land uses constitute productivity and which constitute waste. What does the CCC's classification of the degraded oak savanna as "waste ground" obscure about the prior management history of the site?

2. **Fire as political act:** The fire regime that maintained Oak Openings' open-canopy structure was sustained by Ottawa and Potawatomi land management for centuries before removal. Using the PLI photography, document the visual evidence of prescribed fire in the current restoration program (burn scars, char patterns on bark, abrupt vegetation transitions). Then develop an argument about what it means that the ecological function of Indigenous land management must now be performed by a state institution with a budget line and a burn permit. What is gained and what is lost in that translation?

3. **Succession, displacement, and the century of decline:** The ecological degradation of Oak Openings between the 1833 Treaty of Chicago (removal) and the 1930s CCC intervention spans approximately one century. During that period, fire suppression allowed forest succession to encroach on the sand plain. Map this ecological timeline against the removal history: identify what management practices ended with dispossession, what succession processes accelerated in their absence, and how the CCC's intervention addressed symptoms (degraded land) while leaving the cause (dispossession of the land's managers) unexamined.
